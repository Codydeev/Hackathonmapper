STUHI Hackathon · When GPS fails

Which way did they go?

When a phone drops out of Bluetooth range, the app predicts which way the person went along the paths, with a percentage for each route and how far along it they probably are by now. It learned from where people really walk. These are real walks from the Hervanta forest, which the model never trained on; the dotted line shows what really happened.

    How it decides

    No circles: the model follows the path network and puts a probability on every way the person could have gone.

    1 · Where they are
    Snap to the paths

    The last 60 s of the walk is matched to the nearest paths, including which way they were heading. 79–92% of real walking is on a mapped path.

    2 · At every fork
    Which way?

    A probability for each branch: how sharp the turn is, whether the path continues, path type, dead ends, marked hiking routes, and how many other people walked each branch.

    3 · How far
    Real walking speed

    How far people really get in t minutes, at their recent pace, including stops. Gives the marker and its band.

    4 · Routes
    2–4 options

    All branches are followed out to 10 minutes, then grouped into the routes that really differ, each with its percentage.

    How well it works

    Sharpened by signals

    Limits

    • The walks come from people who recorded their hikes, not from people who were lost. Lost people tend to follow trails, streams and other lines in the terrain.
    • "Where people usually walk" needs walking data for the area. Without it the model still uses the shape of the paths, with less certainty.
    • Someone who leaves the paths is only covered by the "left the paths" percentage, not by a route.
    • The signal results are simulated from real walks with realistic errors. Real compasses in a forest can be worse: with a ±15° compass and ±10% step length the median stayed at 6–8 m, but the 95% radius held only 73–84% of the time.
